Supply Chain and Fulfilment

Where does the delivery date you committed to actually break?

Demand signalThe commitment is created
Delivery confirmedThe commitment is met

Why this process resists visibility

Fulfilment is reported as a percentage and experienced as a series of recoveries that largely go unrecorded.

  • On-time delivery reports the verdict

    It tells you the date was missed. It cannot tell you whether the date was lost in planning, at a supplier, on the warehouse floor or at the carrier.

  • The chain crosses companies as well as systems

    Suppliers, contract manufacturers, third-party logistics providers and carriers each hold part of the record, and much of it arrives late or in a form that is hard to reconcile.

  • Plans overwrite their own history

    Each replan replaces the last, so the original promise and the moment it became undeliverable are often lost from the system that made it.

  • Expediting is an invisible subsidy

    The calls, swaps and premium freight that rescue a date leave little trace, so a process that depends on rescue can look like a process that works.

  • Site variation is defended more often than measured

    Most locations treat their route as a necessary adaptation, and without comparable execution data the claim is difficult to test.

Where time and effort accumulate

The process is normally drawn as six clean stages. Execution data shows what actually collects at each one, and how much of the cycle is spent waiting rather than working.

  1. Stage 1

    Demand and planning

    • Signals arriving late or unstructured
    • Plans re-run after the commitment is made
    • Forecasts overridden manually
    • Safety stock adjusted case by case
  2. Stage 2

    Supply and procurement

    • Supplier confirmations chased by hand
    • Lead times that no longer reflect reality
    • Substitutions approved outside the plan
    • Shortages surfacing at the line
  3. Stage 3

    Production and inbound

    • Schedule changes after release
    • Quality holds and rework loops
    • Unplanned changeovers
    • Receipts posted after the goods arrive
  4. Stage 4

    Warehouse

    • Put-away and picking delays
    • Stock located outside its recorded position
    • Allocation reassigned between orders
    • Counting interrupting outbound flow
  5. Stage 5

    Transport

    • Carrier booking made late
    • Loads consolidated and split after booking
    • Collection windows missed
    • Status updates arriving after the event
  6. Stage 6

    Delivery and confirmation

    • Proof of delivery captured late
    • Failed deliveries and redelivery
    • Short and damaged receipts
    • Returns opened at the point of delivery

What runs underneath all six

These four behaviours recur across the stages and often account for a large share of the cycle: work waiting in a queue without a clear owner, work looping back to a stage it already passed, work changing hands between teams with little record of the handoff, and the same delivery type running through materially different routes depending on who touched it.
